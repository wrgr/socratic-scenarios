Every result is simulation-based mechanism evidence. No learning-gains claim is made; the pre-registered human cohort study is the endpoint this screen de-risks. Live-model numbers are single-model effect sizes.

The problem

You built a model that's supposed to answer from a curated corpus — a rulebook, a set of product facts. It gives a right-looking answer. Did it actually use the corpus, or did it already know? If the corpus is decorative, the moment your rules change the whole thing quietly breaks and you won't see it coming. Finding out by human study is slow, expensive, and confounded. We measure it cheaply, in silico, first.

The instrument

Necessity = ablate a corpus item and measure the change in a scored task outcome — a with-versus-without contrast, not a "did the context influence the output?" score. A raw reading is confounded, though: near-zero could mean already known or never used. That confound is the whole problem, and the next section is how we remove it.

The headline: calibrate by building the ground truth

Teach a fact into the weights along a graded schedule and watch necessity for that fact fall as a dose-response. If it falls smoothly to zero, a near-zero reading is trustworthy evidence the model already knew the item. We calibrate in a simulator-free fictional-fact QA task (invented facts no model can have memorized, scored by an answer checker) — so the method isn't a trick of one hand-built environment.

The caution we found: knowing ≠ doing

Teach the same kind of fact as the basis for a decision (a ship must turn to avoid a hidden charted hazard) and something surprising happens. The model can say the fact perfectly, but its scored decision doesn't budge — it knows but doesn't do. The gap closes only when reasoning is elicited; forcing the behavior by direct fine-tuning backfires into a blanket "hazard everywhere" policy, not the specific fact.

Why it matters for the measure: on a decision objective, whether a taught fact "counts" depends on whether it's elicited — so necessity read off a single-shot decision can under-report knowledge the model actually holds. The instrument must declare which channel it scores.

The payoff: a corpus diagnosis, and the misled blind spot

On the calibrated measure, label every corpus item: relied (changes the decision for the better), redundant (already known — prune it), unusable (present but the model can't act on it — fix the model), or misled. The misled case is the sharp one:

A learner faithfully following a wrong retrieved rule looks maximally reliant — remove the rule and its decision swings hard — yet it is actively harmed. A subtractive necessity test and an influence-based faithfulness score both credit it as heavily used and well-grounded. Only reading necessity as decision-changes × succeeds-with separates faithful compliance with a good rule from faithful compliance with a harmful one. On a live model, a wrong rule ~8×'d the harmful into-the-obstacle maneuver — via confabulation, not a perception failure.
